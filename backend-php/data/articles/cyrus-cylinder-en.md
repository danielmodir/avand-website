The **Cyrus Cylinder** is a baked clay cylinder inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform. It was written after **Cyrus the Great**, founder of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, took the city of Babylon in 539 BCE.

## Where is it?

It was found in 1879 during excavations at Babylon, in today's Iraq, and is kept in the **British Museum** in London. It has been lent to other countries several times, including Iran's National Museum in 2010. A replica stands at the United Nations headquarters in New York.

## What does it say?

The text follows the style of Mesopotamian royal inscriptions. It presents Cyrus as a king who entered Babylon peacefully, and it describes:

- Cyrus, his ancestors and the legitimacy of his rule;
- the people of Babylon being freed from oppression, with the city left in peace;
- displaced peoples being allowed to return to their homes;
- temples being restored and the statues of gods returned to their sanctuaries.

## The first charter of human rights?

In recent decades the cylinder has often been called the **"first charter of human rights"**, as a symbol of tolerance and respect for different peoples and beliefs. Many historians point out that it is not a declaration of rights in the modern sense but a royal building inscription in the Mesopotamian tradition. Still, the return of displaced peoples and respect for local cults, also known from other sources, are well-known features of Cyrus's reign.

## Cyrus and the Achaemenids

Cyrus founded the Achaemenid Empire in the mid-sixth century BCE; under his successors it stretched from the Indus valley to Egypt and Asia Minor. His tomb at **Pasargadae**, in Fars province, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
