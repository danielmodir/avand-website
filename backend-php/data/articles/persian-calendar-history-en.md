The calendar used in Iran today, the **Solar Hijri** calendar, is one of the most accurate calendars in use anywhere. It is the result of thousands of years of Iranians watching the sky and counting days and seasons.

## Ancient Persia

Old Persian inscriptions, such as Darius the Great's inscription at **Behistun**, date events with month names that differ from today's. Most scholars think the Achaemenid administrative calendar was **lunisolar**, like the Babylonian one: months began with the new moon, and an extra month was added every few years to keep the year in step with the seasons.

## The Zoroastrian calendar

Alongside it grew the calendar now called **Zoroastrian** or Avestan: twelve months of 30 days, plus five extra days at the end of the year (the *Gathas*), for 365 days. Months and days were named after Avestan divine beings and ideas, the same names still used for the months today: Farvardin, Ordibehesht, Khordad and so on. When a day's name matched its month's name, it was a festival; **Mehregan**, for example, fell on the day of Mehr in the month of Mehr.

## The Jalali calendar

In the Seljuk era, under Sultan Malik-Shah, a group of astronomers that included **Omar Khayyam** devised a new calendar, called *Jalali* after the sultan (1079 CE). Its year began exactly at the **March equinox**, so Nowruz always fell on the right day and the calendar never drifted away from the seasons.

## Today's Solar Hijri calendar

In 1925 (1304 in the Persian calendar), Iran's parliament made the Solar Hijri calendar official, with the old Persian month names. Its epoch is the Hijra of the Prophet of Islam, but its year is solar and, like the Jalali calendar, starts at Nowruz:

- the first six months (Farvardin to Shahrivar) have 31 days;
- the next five (Mehr to Bahman) have 30;
- Esfand has 29 days, or 30 in a leap year.

Because the start of each year is set by the real moment of the equinox rather than a fixed leap-year rule, the calendar year stays in step with the solar year.
